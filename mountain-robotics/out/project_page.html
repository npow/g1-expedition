The problem

The load is not heavy. It is long.

A conifer comes down across the fixed-line route above base camp. It weighs about 11 kg, well inside what a G1 can hold.

But no single humanoid can move it. A robot gripping a 1.7 m log anywhere except its centre of mass cannot keep it level, and a robot gripping it at the centre has no leverage over its yaw. That is the actual reason cooperative manipulation exists, and it is why this is a two-robot problem rather than one robot doing it twice.

Both machines stand at either end, clip both hands into rope slings choked around the trunk, and work from a single shared plan.

Why it suits extreme conditions
  • The load is unknown. Nobody hands a mountain robot a spec sheet. The team weighs the log through its own slings and infers where its centre of mass sits from how the load splits.
  • The answer is sometimes no. A go/no-go check on measured load against arm capacity is the decision a rope team actually makes.
  • Conditions degrade mid-task. Gusts, verglas underfoot and an off-centre load all arrive while the thing is already in the air.
  • Failure has to be graceful. Every abort is a controlled set-down from wherever the load currently is — never a drop.

Coordination

One virtual object, not two arm plans

The robots are never given independent trajectories. The controller maintains a single commanded payload pose, and all four palm targets are that pose composed with each hand’s fixed sling-loop offset. Because every hand derives from one moving frame, the team is kinematically consistent by construction: no leader, no follower, and no way for the two robots to disagree about where the log is going.

commanded, feed-forward measured force, feeds the mission clock

Each hand is clipped to the payload by a 3-DoF equality/connect — the right abstraction for a carabiner in a sling, since the load is carried but the hand is free to rotate. It also makes the solver’s Lagrange multipliers a direct readout of the force each hand transmits, so the whole safety layer reads true interaction forces with no added sensors.

The learned part

The policy outputs a residual on a controller that already works — per robot: palm height left and right, CoM bias in x and y, squat depth. Ten numbers, each worth ±3.5 cm, expressed in the coordinator’s own units rather than reaching around it to the joints.

Two reasons for that shape. There is always a working baseline to fall back on, so the demo cannot end with nothing to show. And the comparison that matters — with and without the policy, same seed, same gust, same patch of ice — is a controlled experiment rather than two anecdotes.

Trained with PPO on Hugging Face Jobs — 5.0M environment steps, 1395 updates, ~85 minutes on an A10G. The export is plain numpy arrays, so the demo laptop needs no PyTorch.

It did not work, and that is the finding. The policy rescued 2 episodes the baseline lost and lost 4 it won — inside the noise, pointing the wrong way. Episode return is dominated by which disturbance was drawn, not by what the policy did: a ±3.5 cm palm correction moves the return far less than the gap between a 2 N gust and a 14 N one, so the advantage estimate is mostly measuring the weather. Train on the failure mode alone, cut the episode-level variance, and give the residual authority where the failures actually are — in that order.

What the engineering cost the interesting parts were the things that did not work

Menagerie’s G1 falls over in three seconds

Stock scene, stock keyframes, no controller: the pelvis travels 0.9 m and ends at 0.1 m height. Position servos alone do not stabilise an underactuated biped — which is why the Playground models ship paired with learned policies. Standing had to be built, not assumed.

survives3 s→ whole mission

The IK collapsed the robot in a quarter of a second

Pinning the feet to a stale world-frame stance creates a feedback loop: the pelvis sags a few millimetres under load, the solver sees feet that dropped with it and “corrects” by retracting the legs, which lowers the pelvis further. Refreshing the foot targets each tick makes those rows a pure constraint.

pelvis after 0.6 s0.06 m0.72 m

Stiffer arms made it worse

Raising arm gains to match the legs put an 8 Hz resonance between the arm servos and the compliant sling straight through the carry. With the controller frozen, sling force still rang ±60 N about a 108 N load. Holding a compliant load is a case where the softer joint is the more stable one.

force std, kp 270 → 14591 N → 3.9 N

Half the load was invisible

The weigh-in read 5.6 kg for an 11 kg log. Slings are not the only path into the payload — a hand cupped under a log carries part of it by touching it, and that share appears in no equality multiplier. Adding contact forces fixed the weigh-in, and because balance uses the same number, it also lifted the randomised success rate and turned verglas at μ=0.45 from a fall into a completed mission.

weigh-in on 11 kg5.6 → 10.9 kgsuccess 43% → 71%

The reward paid the agent to give up

Dense penalties against a small survival bonus made a whole episode return about −400, so quitting was the best move on the board: tip a robot at step 300 and you skip 460 steps of penalty. Training went backwards exactly as predicted. The fix is to make a well-tracked step score positive and charge an abort for the rest of the episode it threw away.

success while training71% → 58%return −400 → +620

The obvious fix was the wrong one

Two humanoids holding one rigid body form a closed kinematic chain, so it looks obvious that the carry needs PD feedback on the measured payload pose. It does not. Every nonzero gain made the hold shorter, monotonically. The code is still in the tree at zero gain, because the negative result is worth more than the code.

hold duration6.8 s → 1.9 sas gain rises
Limitations, stated plainly
  • The feet never move. Everything here is standing manipulation. The lateral work envelope is about ±15 cm; going further needs stepping.
  • The carry has a ten-second stability window. Internal force in the closed chain eventually pulls a robot off balance. The principled fix — explicit internal-force regulation — is the first thing to build next.
  • Grip is a constraint, not contact. Hands clip to sling loops rather than closing fingers on a rope. Force-limited and breakable, but not a dexterous grasp.
  • The arm capacity number is the soft one. 60 N per hand is the usable force for a braced two-handed hold. It is the first assumption to check against hardware, and the go/no-go threshold moves with it.
  • Simulation only. No hardware transfer attempted. The G1 model is the one MuJoCo Playground transferred to hardware, which is why it was chosen.
